1The problem

A simulated online shop sends us three shoppers every second. For each one we have three seconds to pick 10 products for a carousel. The shopper may click one and may buy it.

A hidden model decides how likely that is, and our score is how close our 10 products come to the 10 that model would have picked itself. We never see the hidden model. We only see who the shopper is, what they looked at in the last minutes, what happened to our past answers, and a public leaderboard.

2The idea: ask the simulator questions

We do not try to copy the hidden model. We ask it questions.

We look at the data and write down what seems to matter. We turn each belief into a hypothesis: one sentence that can be proven wrong, and the numbers that would prove it. We build a strategy from the beliefs that survived, and let strategies compete on live traffic, side by side, each on its own slice of shoppers.

Everything we learn goes into one page, docs/SIMULATOR.md. Strategies are built from that page, so every line of a strategy has a reason we can point to.

3The pieces

The router is the only program that answers shoppers. Every fraction of a second it reads the new requests, decides which strategy answers each one, checks the answer and sends it. The state is what the router knows right now: the catalogue, what is out of stock, what is trending in the last 15 minutes, and what each shopper bought and viewed.

The guard removes anything out of stock or repeated and fills the gaps with best sellers, so a broken experiment cannot produce an invalid answer. arms.toml is the one switch: which strategies share the shoppers for each kind of request, and how many shoppers go to the explore arm. The trainer re-fits our learned model, the ranker, on the outcomes of our own answers every 20 minutes. RawTree is the memory.

Since 18:10 UTC on 14 November the router runs on a small cloud machine, a VM, as a service that restarts by itself. The laptop only trains the models and pushes each new one to the VM.

4How one answer is made

A shopper on a product page has looked at two smartwatches. The rules2 strategy collects candidates and gives each one a score: the appeal of the product, times how close it is to what the shopper is doing now, times whether they looked at it before, times how much they like its category, times how much their device and region like it, times a trend boost, times a price-cut boost.

Products the shopper already bought are removed. The 10 highest scores are the answer, best first. Each multiplier is an effect size we measured: a product from the shopper's recent views sells about 11 times better than one from another category, so its multiplier is 12.

The first version, rules, took its weights from the shop's old logs. We measured them again on live feedback and they changed, because history had them wrong by 3 to 10 times: a recent view was worth 35 and is now 12, and the favourite category counts ten times more than before. A past view and the session do not multiply: the larger one counts, times 1.4.

5What the shopper's session tells us

Our first answers were the same best-seller list for every shopper, so any difference in purchases comes from the shopper, not from the list. Measured on slots 1–3, the product the shopper just looked at converts about 35 times better than a product from another category. That first reading rested on 91 impressions. With twenty times more data, and over all slots, the gap settled at about 11 times.

On product and category pages, 89% of what the best-seller list showed was from another category. That is the gap our session strategies close: rules2, rules4 and the ranker.

6Three kinds of request, each with its own strategies

Not every request is the same problem. An ordinary request comes from a returning shopper who looked at something in the last minutes: we know their history and their session. A new shopper with views has a session but no history. A request with no recent views is the home page: there is no session to read at all.

The router looks at the request, decides which kind it is, and picks from the list of arms that arms.toml gives for that kind. A strategy that is good with a session can be poor without one, so each kind is tested and decided on its own.

The home page was our leak. About 0.4% of those requests ended in a purchase, below the baseline's 0.7% over all requests. cold2 targets it. It puts first the products the shopper once added to the cart and never bought, then what they viewed earlier today, then best sellers from their top category. Cart products made 16 of the 24 home purchases of returning shoppers.

7How we know what works: side by side

Clicks and purchases on our own answers are too rare to compare two strategies quickly: about 75 purchases an hour over all traffic. The leaderboard is sharper, because it scores the chance of a purchase and not the purchase itself.

Since 15:50 UTC on Saturday the leaderboard scores each model version on its own, for any window from 15 minutes to 3 hours. So we split the shoppers. A hash of the user id puts each shopper in one slice, each slice is answered by one strategy, and each strategy appears as its own line on the leaderboard. The arms run at the same moment, so they see the same shop, the same trends and the same hour of the day.

We compare only within the same kind of request. A strategy answering new shoppers will always look better than one answering the home page, whatever it does.

The leaderboard is public, so our strategies appear under mineral names that mean nothing: quartz is the baseline, mica explore, flint rules, slate rules2, basalt rules3, opal cold1, jasper the ranker, agate cold2 and onyx rules4. A suffix marks the kind of request: -n for new shoppers, -h for no recent views. Other teams learn nothing from the names.

A challenger becomes champion when it is ahead in two 30-minute windows in a row, with about 1,000 requests per arm.

Our first method was different. The leaderboard used to give one number for the whole last hour, so strategies took turns in 10-minute blocks and we subtracted two readings ten minutes apart to isolate one block. It worked, but each arm ran at a different time, half the traffic was spent on taking turns, and one reading carried about 1 score point of noise. The direct reading replaced it.

8The model that learns from our own feedback

The rule strategies use weights we measured by hand, one at a time. The ranker learns them all together. It is a standard learning method, gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM), trained on the outcomes of our own answers: every product we showed, and whether it was clicked.

For each candidate it reads 21 inputs: the product's appeal, popularity and price, how it relates to the session, whether the shopper viewed it before, the shopper's share of views in its category, the segment, the page, the device. It predicts the chance of a click. We then multiply by the chance that a click becomes a purchase, which we measured live: 81% for a recent view, 27% for anything else.

It is trained again every 20 minutes, so it follows the shop as the day goes on. The explore arm matters here. A model can only learn about products that were shown, and explore brings outcomes on products the champion never shows.

The first model went live at 16:10 UTC on 601,187 impressions and 1,794 clicks. Tested on the newest fifth of the data, which it had not seen, it put a clicked product above an unclicked one 78% of the time, against 71% for the slot alone. At 16:35 UTC it became champion, after leading rules2 in two 30-minute windows: 1.54% against 1.29% for returning shoppers and 2.41% against 2.29% for new shoppers.

Its successor, ranker2, has been champion since 18:28 UTC. It reads 34 inputs, adds the shopper's carts and their taste for the words in product descriptions, and learns the chance that a click becomes a purchase instead of using the fixed 81% and 27%. ranker8 is ranker2 with its noisiest inputs removed and the rank from a rule strategy, rules5, added as an input. It is the one that does best for new shoppers.

9Why a little traffic is random

The shop's old logs only show what the old carousel showed: popular products from one category. They cannot tell us what happens when we show a shopper something from another category, or a brand-new product.

The explore arm shows a few shoppers products we would not normally pick, from six groups, and records the chance each product had of being shown. That is how we measure what the logs cannot.

10History is a prior, live is the truth

The shop's 60-day history log and the live shop do not always agree. In the history log, a shopper is about 19 times more likely to buy a product they already bought. Live, an already-bought product does worse than one they never saw.

So every effect from history is a prior, a starting guess, until we see it live. A model trained on history alone would push already-bought products. Every strategy of ours removes them.

On 14 November the organisers made this the clearest example. They clarified that the history log and the shop's live organic events were generated without the repeat-purchase penalty, so repeat purchases seen there mean nothing. Our own feedback follows the penalty: a shopper who already bought a product is much less likely to buy it again when we show it. Our live counts were small, and the clarification settled it.

The same lesson came back with the weights of section 4, which history had wrong by 3 to 10 times, and with the ranker, which is trained on live feedback only.

11What must never happen

Two programs answering at once: a lease in RawTree stops a second router from starting. An invalid answer: the guard prevents it.

A strategy that only works on the practice shoppers. The final has new shoppers, new products and new trends, so strategies score products by their attributes and shoppers by their session and segment. What we remember about a particular shopper is a bonus.

12Who built it: one developer and a team of AI agents

One developer built quick2query. The work was done with Claude Code, an AI assistant that writes and runs code. The developer decided what to try; AI agents did most of the building, measuring and watching. Each agent had one job.

AgentWhat it did
Main sessionDecides, plans and keeps the journal, docs/JOURNAL.md. Every promotion and every retirement is a dated line there.
BuildersSubagents that write the pieces whose behaviour is fixed and testable: strategies, trainers, the offline replay, hypothesis tests.
Fresh eyesAnalysts that get only the tables and the problem statement, not our beliefs, and report what they find. They found the category lock (lists that stay in the category of the last product viewed sell more) and each shopper's purchase taste.
Insight workersMany agents in parallel, each answering one open question with SQL. See below.
Night operatorscripts/night_operator.py, a loop that ran with nobody watching. Every 15 minutes it read the score of each arm, started 150-minute trials from a queue, and retired arms that fell clearly behind, measured in standard errors (a z-score). It never dropped the champion.

Insight workers (scripts/insight_worker.py, src/q2q/memory.py) are several Claude Code agents that run in parallel with no one at the keyboard. Each one claims an open question, stored as a row in team_01.scratch. It answers it only by running read-only SQL in RawTree, through the RawTree MCP server, the connector that lets an agent query RawTree directly. It writes its finding and a verdict back as a row, and adds its follow-up questions as new rows.

The claim makes it safe to run many workers on many machines: a question goes to whoever claimed it first. The memory is RawTree rows, so every agent and the developer see the same questions and answers. The 26 past hypotheses (docs/hypotheses/) are loaded into the same memory, marked as a backfill.

Three real findings from the insight workers on the morning of 15 November, in their own numbers:

Why this goes beyond one shop. Any stream whose data drifts needs the same loop: device telemetry, logs, prices. Watch the stream, ask a question, test the answer on a small share, adapt. Only the SQL and the strategy files change.
