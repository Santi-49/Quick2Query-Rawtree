1The problem

A simulated online shop sends us three shoppers every second. For each one we have three seconds to pick 10 products for a carousel. The shopper may click one and may buy it.

A hidden model decides how likely that is, and our score is how close our 10 products come to the 10 that model would have picked itself. We never see the hidden model. We only see who the shopper is, what they looked at in the last minutes, what happened to our past answers, and a public leaderboard.

2The idea: ask the simulator questions

We do not try to copy the hidden model. We ask it questions.

We look at the data and write down what seems to matter. We turn each belief into a hypothesis: one sentence that can be proven wrong, and the numbers that would prove it. We build a strategy from the beliefs that survived, and let strategies compete on live traffic, ten minutes each.

Everything we learn goes into one page, docs/SIMULATOR.md. Strategies are built from that page, so every line of a strategy has a reason we can point to.

3The pieces

The router is the only program that answers shoppers. Every fraction of a second it reads the new requests, decides which strategy answers each one, checks the answer and sends it. The state is what the router knows right now: the catalogue, what is out of stock, what is trending in the last 15 minutes, and what each shopper bought and viewed.

The guard removes anything out of stock or repeated and fills the gaps with best sellers, so a broken experiment cannot produce an invalid answer. arms.toml is the one switch: which strategy owns which 10-minute block, and how many shoppers go to the explore arm. RawTree is the memory.

4How one answer is made

A shopper on a product page has looked at two smartwatches. The rules strategy collects candidates and gives each one a score: the appeal of the product, times how close it is to what the shopper is doing now, times whether they looked at it before, times how much they like its category, times how much their device and region like it, times a trend boost, times a price-cut boost.

Products the shopper already bought are removed. The 10 highest scores are the answer, best first. Each multiplier is an effect size we measured: the product being looked at converts about 35 times better than one from another category, so its multiplier is 35.

5What the shopper's session tells us

Our first answers were the same best-seller list for every shopper, so any difference in purchases comes from the shopper, not from the list. Measured on slots 1–3, the product the shopper just looked at converts about 35 times better than a product from another category.

On product and category pages, 89% of what the best-seller list showed was from another category. That is the gap rules closes.

6How we know what works: 10-minute blocks

Clicks and purchases on our own answers are too rare to compare two strategies quickly: about 75 purchases an hour over all traffic.

The leaderboard gives an exact number, but for the whole last hour. Two readings ten minutes apart differ only by the ten minutes that entered and the ten that left. Subtracting them gives our result for a single 10-minute block, and the same subtraction for the baseline team compares us on the very same shoppers.

So strategies take turns. A challenger becomes champion after winning three pairs of blocks.

7Why a little traffic is random

The shop's old logs only show what the old carousel showed: popular products from one category. They cannot tell us what happens when we show a shopper something from another category, or a brand-new product.

The explore arm shows a few shoppers products we would not normally pick, from six groups, and records the chance each product had of being shown. That is how we measure what the logs cannot.

8History is a prior, live is the truth

The shop's 60-day history log and the live shop do not always agree. In the history log, a shopper is about 19 times more likely to buy a product they already bought. Live, an already-bought product does worse than one they never saw.

So every effect from history is a prior, a starting guess, until we see it live. A model trained on history alone would push already-bought products. rules removes them. The live counts are still small, so a probe will confirm it.

9What must never happen

Two programs answering at once: a lease in RawTree stops a second router from starting. An invalid answer: the guard prevents it.

A strategy that only works on the practice shoppers. The final has new shoppers, new products and new trends, so strategies score products by their attributes and shoppers by their session and segment. What we remember about a particular shopper is a bonus.
